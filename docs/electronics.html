Documentation · Electronics

Electronics

There are two ways to give Marvin something to think with. One you can solder this weekend from four modules and a reel of wire. The other is two purpose-designed boards that nobody has drawn yet — what follows for those is a specification, not a product.

Start here

The two paths

They share the chassis, the motors, the servos and the firmware. They differ in what sits between the battery and the motors — and in whether you can order it today.

Path A

Works today

Solder it yourself

Four breakout modules, joined by hand-soldered wire, tucked into the chassis. This is what the prototype in the videos is. It drives, it looks around, and it answers over Bluetooth.

Path B

Specification only

The Marvin PCBs

Two boards shaped to the chassis: one in the head, one in the base, joined by a six-wire harness through the neck. They do not exist. There is no schematic, no layout and no Gerber file — only the specification set out below.

The firmware does not care which one you build. Every pin lives in config.h, so moving from the C3 on Path A to the S3 on Path B is a configuration change rather than a rewrite. The assembly sequence is written for both, and switches between them.

Notes 03.3 — Things that catch people out

  1. 01

    Motor B is wired inverted, on purpose

    IN3 and IN4 are swapped so that a positive speed on both channels drives forward, without the firmware correcting per side. Wire it the obvious way and one track runs backwards.

  2. 02

    All four motor pins are driven LOW at startup

    Before anything else, because ESP32 pins float during boot and a floating H-bridge input makes the tracks lurch.

  3. 03

    A weak battery browns the board out

    If Marvin connects over Bluetooth and then drops the moment the motors draw current, the supply is sagging. It is almost never the firmware. This is the single largest reason Path B exists.

Path B · Architecture

Two boards, six wires

The chassis decides the architecture. The head has room for a module, a battery and two displays; the base has the motors and a view of the floor; and between them is a joint that rotates, which is the last place you want a loom of analogue sensor lines.

So the split is by function, not by convenience. The main board in the head runs Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, owns power, and drives the displays and the forward-looking sensors. The base board takes a cheap RISC-V microcontroller and gives it one job: generate motor PWM, watch the floor, and shout if the floor disappears.

They talk over I²C, main as master and base as slave, which reduces the joint to six conductors: two for power, one ground, two for the bus and one interrupt. Cliff detection cannot afford to wait for a poll, so it gets that dedicated line.

The consequence worth noticing: the base board is not a dumb motor shield. It keeps the tracks turning at the right rate and stops the robot driving off a table even while the ESP32 is busy with something else.

Division of labour

BoardWhereOwns
MainHeadRadio, power, charging, displays, TOF, IMU, IR, head servo
PeripheralBaseTrack motors, base servo, four floor sensors

One I²C master, one slave at 0x30, and a hardware interrupt that bypasses the bus entirely.

Path B · Head electronics

Main board — head

High-level logic, the radio, all of the power management, both displays and the forward-looking sensors.

Core and system logic

MCU module
ESP32-S3-WROOM-1-N8R2 — dual-core LX7, 8 MB flash, 2 MB PSRAM, PCB antenna
USB
16-pin USB Type-C with 5.1 kΩ CC pull-downs — power in, and native USB CDC/JTAG for programming and debug
Protection
USBLC6-2SC6 ESD array on D+/D−
Switches
Tactile RESET and BOOT (IO0)

Power

Charger
ETA6002 or TP4056 linear charger, 500 mA–1 A, from USB-C VBUS into a 2-pin JST-PH 2.0 mm battery header
3.3 V rail
SY8089AAC 2 A buck, 3.0–4.2 V in. Feeds the ESP32-S3, both ST7789s, the TOF, the IMU and the base board's logic
Boost rail
MT3608 step-up, 5.0–6.0 V, dedicated to the head servo and the base board's motors and servo. A Boost_EN line cuts it during sleep
Telemetry
100 kΩ / 100 kΩ divider from the battery positive terminal to an ADC pin

Peripherals and interfaces

Distance
VL53L1X time-of-flight, I²C 0x29, on the forward board edge — objects to 4 m
Motion
LSM6DS3TR-C 6-axis gyro and accelerometer, I²C 0x6A
Infrared
940 nm high-power LED driven by an AO3400 N-channel MOSFET (IR_TX), with an IRM-3638T 38 kHz demodulating receiver (IR_RX) — so two Marvins can notice each other
Displays
2 × 0.5 mm-pitch FPC ZIF connectors, 8- or 10-pin, for naked 1.14″ ST7789 panels. Shared SPI bus, one chip-select each
Head servo
3-pin 2.54 mm or JST-XH surface-mount header — V_BOOST, GND, SERVO_PWM1

The two panels are the eyes. They are the reason the head is the board that carries the processor, rather than the base.

Path B · Base electronics

Peripheral board — base

Motor driving, floor sensing and base actuation, run by a microcontroller that costs less than the connector it plugs into.

Co-processor

Sub-MCU
WCH CH32V003F4P6, TSSOP-20, RISC-V core at 48 MHz
Role
I²C slave at 0x30. Polls the floor sensors continuously, generates the high-frequency PWM for the DC motors and the base servo, and asserts an interrupt to the ESP32 the moment it sees a drop
Programming
1-pin SWIO header for factory flashing

Motors and actuation

Driver
TI DRV8833 or equivalent AT8833 dual H-bridge, 1.5 A RMS per channel
VMOT
The V_BOOST 5.0–6.0 V rail from the main board
VCC
The 3.3 V rail
Wheel motors
2 × 2-pin JST-ZH 1.5 mm, or solder pads, for the N20 gear motors. Four PWM channels from the CH32V003 — AIN1/AIN2 and BIN1/BIN2 — give forward, reverse and brake
Base servo
3-pin 2.54 mm header — V_BOOST, GND, SERVO_PWM2, timed by the CH32V003

Floor and cliff sensing

Sensors
4 × Everlight ITR8307/TR8 or Vishay TCRT5000 reflective optical sensors, one at each corner of the base
Emitters
Driven from the 3.3 V rail through current-limiting resistors, switchable by a single N-FET so an idle robot stops paying for them
Receivers
Phototransistor outputs into four dedicated ADC channels on the CH32V003
Trigger
An internal threshold raises BASE_INT when a corner sees a void — the edge of a table

Why a second microcontroller

Because a table edge does not wait for the radio stack. Cliff detection is a hard real-time job with a very short deadline, and giving it its own processor and its own interrupt line means the answer does not depend on what the ESP32 happens to be doing.

Path B · Interconnect

Board to board

One harness bridges the two boards. Six conductors, chosen so that no analogue sensor array and no individual motor PWM line ever has to cross a joint that rotates.

1V_BOOST5.0–6.0 V · 2 APower for the N20s and the base servo

Connector and cable

Connector
6-pin JST-SH 1.0 mm, or JST-GH 1.25 mm, right-angle surface-mount — the same part at both ends
Cable
6-core highly flexible silicone ribbon. The head articulates, and ordinary stranded ribbon work-hardens and fails at the joint
Route
Up through the neck opening, before the neck is bolted down — see the PCB assembly sequence

This is the pre-crimped cable in the bill of materials. It is the one part of Path B you can buy today, because the crimping is the part nobody wants to do by hand.

Path B · Production

Manufacturing

The specification was costed against a turnkey assembler's catalogue, which is why the part choices look conservative. They are: every one of them is a stock part somebody else already places by the reel.

Stackup
Both boards 2-layer or 4-layer FR4, 1.6 mm, 1 oz copper
Parts
Every component — the ESP32 module, CH32V003, MT3608, SY8089, motor driver, connectors — from an assembler's basic and extended libraries
Assembly
100 % turnkey SMT. Headers and ZIF connectors are specified as surface-mount so there is no hand through-hole work in a 100–1000 unit run
Supplier
Costed against JLCPCB; nothing here depends on it. Any assembler with an equivalent stock library will do

There is one part of Marvin that no 3D printer and no assembler will make for you, and it is not a board.

The face

The front glass is the only part that needs a contract manufacturer: laser-cut, screen-printed, ideally in a toughened glass. Plenty of shops on Alibaba quote both operations together in small runs.

Until you order one, print the face plate in black PLA. It is the same outline and it hides the same electronics — see the print section.

What Path B still needs

In order, and none of it started

  1. A schematic for each board, drawn against this specification
  2. A measured current budget — the numbers above are chosen, not verified
  3. A layout for each board that fits inside part 01 and part 05
  4. Firmware for the CH32V003, and an I²C register map the ESP32 can talk to
  5. One fabricated set, and an honest account of what was wrong with it
