Meet Marvin

What it is

Marvin drives on two rubber tracks and looks around with a head that pans and tilts on two servos. That is the whole of its body language, and it turns out to be enough.

It is published as open source hardware: firmware, electronics, mechanical design, and the app you drive it with. The purpose is reproducibility. Anyone with a 3D printer and a soldering iron should be able to build one.

It is also an early prototype. The drivetrain, the head and the Bluetooth link work today, driven by four breakout modules soldered together by hand. The sensing and the eyes do not exist yet: they belong to a pair of circuit boards that have been specified and not drawn.

That is the fork every builder meets. Solder it yourself and it works this weekend; build the boards and it gains eyes, distance, an IMU and the sense not to drive off a table. The schedule opposite is the honest version, and it doubles as a list of things worth doing.

Behaviour

It is curious

The personality is not a claim made in marketing copy. It is a hand-written table of movements in the firmware, and the comments in that file are the truest statement of the project's character that exists.

The patterns are meant to feel organic: the head often moves before the body turns; pauses with head sweeps simulate curiosity; asymmetric durations avoid a metronomic feel.

firmware/demo.cpp

The head servos hold a target angle and walk the current angle toward it in small steps on a timer. That interpolation is about ten lines of code, and it is the entire reason the movement reads as organic rather than mechanical.

One aside, and then we will let it go: the name nods to the perpetually depressed robot from The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. The resemblance ends at the name. This one is curious, which is more interesting and is what the firmware actually does.

Start building

Build one

Nine parts to print, six to buy, and one decision to make before any of it: how you want the electronics.

The decision

Path A · Works today Solder it yourself An ESP32-C3 SuperMini, a DRV8833, a charger and a step-up converter, joined with wire. Drive, head and Bluetooth, this weekend. Wiring diagram Path B · Specification only The Marvin PCBs Two boards, one in the head and one in the base. They add the eyes and the senses — and they have not been designed yet. Read the specification

Then, either way

First Print Eight parts in PLA on one plate, plus the track links in TPU. Sliced projects included for two Bambu machines. Settings and files Next Assemble Bushings, motors, wheels, neck, head. Fifteen steps soldered, seventeen with the boards; the sequence switches between them. The step sequence Then Flash One script, then a serial monitor at 115200 baud and a Marvin> prompt. Bluetooth starts advertising by itself. Firmware

The steps are written; the step drawings are not. That is the next useful thing anyone could do.

Open source

Open, in the ordinary sense of the word

Not source-available. Properly licensed, with the conventional open-hardware split: permissive for the code, strongly reciprocal for the hardware.

CoversLicence
Software — firmware, controller app, this websiteMIT
Hardware — mechanical design, electronicsCERN-OHL-S-2.0

CERN-OHL-S is reciprocal: build and distribute a modified Marvin and you share your design changes too.

Four ways in

FirmwareEmbedded C++, PlatformIO, one class per peripheral. SoftwareThe controller app and this site. No build step, deliberately. MechanicsCAD is the master. Meshes are derived, never edited. ElectronicsTwo boards, fully specified and entirely undrawn. The largest open invitation.
